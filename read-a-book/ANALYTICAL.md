# Analytical reading

The [`read-a-book`](SKILL.md) branch for 精读: Adler's fifteen rules in three stages. Inspection is already done; its kind, unity, and skeleton are your starting draft. Run the stages in order: each stage's record is the next stage's input.

## Stage 1: structure (what is the book about as a whole?)

1. **Classify** the book by kind and subject matter (refine the inspection's call).
2. **Unity**: state what the whole book is about in one or two sentences.
3. **Outline**: list the major parts in order and show how each serves the unity; outline each part one level down the same way.
4. **Problems**: state the questions the author set out to answer, the main one first.

Done when: the outline covers every chapter, and every part maps to the unity or to one of the problems.

## Stage 2: interpretation (what is being said, and how?)

5. **Come to terms**: find the key words (the index and the author's own emphasis point to them) and pin down the sense the author uses each in. A term is a word plus one meaning; note where the author shifts meaning.
6. **Propositions**: find the most important sentences and state what each asserts, in your own words. A proposition you can only quote, not restate, is one you have not yet understood.
7. **Arguments**: reconstruct each main argument as premises leading to a conclusion, from sentences that may be spread across pages. Note which premises are assumed and which are established.
8. **Solutions**: for each problem from stage 1, record solved, not solved, or acknowledged by the author as unsolved.

Done when: every term, proposition, and argument carries a location, and every stage-1 problem has a status.

## Stage 3: criticism (is it true? what of it?)

The gate: criticize only once stages 1 and 2 are complete and you can honestly say _I understand_. Then take one of three positions per major claim: **agree**, **disagree**, or **suspend judgment**, each backed by reasons, and keep knowledge separate from opinion.

**Etiquette**: argue to find what is true, not to win. A disagreement is worth recording only if it can be settled by reasons or evidence.

**Grounds for disagreeing**: name which one applies.

- **Uninformed**: the author lacks knowledge relevant to the problem.
- **Misinformed**: the author asserts what is not the case.
- **Illogical**: the reasoning does not follow, or is inconsistent.
- **Incomplete**: the analysis stops short, or misses distinctions it needed.

The first three, once shown, oblige you to reject the conclusion they touch; the fourth only limits it. Without one of the first three, you are bound to agree at least in part.

Then answer **what of it?**: what follows if the book is right, for the user, for the field, for what to read next.

Done when: every major claim has a position, every disagreement names its ground with evidence, and _what of it?_ has a concrete answer.

## Imaginative literature

For novels, plays, and poems, the stages keep their shape but change content:

- **Structure**: the kind (novel, play, lyric); the plot summarized in a few sentences as the unity; the sequence of episodes as the outline.
- **Interpretation**: the characters and events stand where terms and propositions stood; the world of the story replaces the argument. Read it on its own terms before asking what it means.
- **Criticism**: judge whether the work succeeds at what it attempts and what it made you feel, before whether you agree with it. Truth here is plausibility within the story's world.
